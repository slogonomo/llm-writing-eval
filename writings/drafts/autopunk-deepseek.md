# Beat Sheet — WHALES

*Feature film, approximately 110 pages*

1. **Opening Image (p. 1)**
Nia, fifteen, floats in the Pacific Northwest shallows surrounded by Bali's pod, her translation rig turning orca calls into half-formed sentences she has spent seven years improving. She belongs to two families at once and believes she will never have to choose between them.

2. **Theme Stated (p. 5)**
Watching the pod surface, Nia's mother explains why their community is always moving: "Sometimes people just have to go." Nia reads it as a fact about other people. It is the sentence that will define her year.

3. **Set-Up (pp. 1–10)**
Nia's life: translation work woven into learning, a techie friend from outside the community, walking biobotic houses her mother designed, and a father who is a blank — he left without goodbye when Nia was small. Agitated pod members approach Nia: Bali is silent, unseen for days, even to long-range echolocation.

4. **Catalyst (p. 12)**
Nia's friend pulls orbital satellite footage: an unmarked tanker descended over open water and scooped the matriarch into a flooded hold, separating her from the pod in minutes. The kidnapping is real, and Nia's scanners cannot touch it.

5. **Debate (pp. 12–25)**
Nia demands action. Her mother refuses: "There's nothing we can do. Sometimes people, and whales, just have to go." Nia's "Not without saying goodbye" is the first time she fights the philosophy she has been raised inside. Confined by her mother, she packs supplies anyway, and the pod — which understands exactly what is happening — stages a diversion so she and her friend can run.

6. **Break into Two (p. 25)**
At the space elevator, Nia says goodbye to the pod and presses a communicator into Bali's absence, telling them to turn it on "if they want it." She boards the lift with a promise: "Don't worry, we'll find her." The quest leaves Earth.

7. **B Story (p. 30)**
Her mother's embodied ASI catches up at the orbital port — comic, superintelligent, and legally forbidden to give answers. With a weathered spacer who knows which worlds can hold whales, Nia gains her real second family for the journey: people who stay near her not because they have to, but because they choose to.

8. **Fun and Games (pp. 30–55)**
World-hopping: a lunar tank of thrill-seeking dolphins who can't get enough of travel, a low-gravity humpback sea whose residents refuse to move, an ice world whose whales remember the tanker's call sign. Nia wins each clue by listening instead of forcing human meaning onto whale speech — and never notices the corporate recovery craft quietly keeping pace, learning everything she learns.

9. **Midpoint (p. 55)**
On the unnamed world, Nia finds a walking house identical to her mother's design — impossible, since hers was built in the Pacific Northwest. Inside: photographs of her mother, of Bali, and one of the three of them, Nia at four, her mother young, and him. The man who flies the tanker is the father who left. The captor and the wound are the same person, and he is coming home.

10. **Bad Guys Close In (pp. 55–75)**
Her father arrives; the reunion is confusion and resentment, and Nia storms out — then uses his maps to locate the secluded ocean where Bali is held and disables the habitat locks herself. Every adult's secrecy now reads to her as cowardice, so she trusts none of it, unaware that her trail is what the pursuing industrialists have been waiting for.

11. **All Is Lost (p. 75)**
Bali will not leave. "She wanted to come here," her father says; the matriarch contacted him first. Bali chose the work over her matriline the way Nia's father chose it over his family — and because Nia led the industrialists to his door, that work is now dying. The kidnapping she crossed space to undo was never a kidnapping, and her rescue attempt may destroy everyone she came to save.

12. **Dark Night of the Soul (pp. 75–85)**
Nia sits with the symmetry: two parents who went, one of them a whale, and her mother's old words — "sometimes people just have to go" — sounding less like a refusal and more like a hard-won truth. She can take Bali by force and lose the father she just found, or let go of the rescue and lose the matriarch she came for. Both feel like abandonment.

13. **Break into Three (p. 85)**
Nia chooses the third way: she will not take Bali, and she will not lose her. She will protect the choice and the work, and she will carry the message home — the relay is built, the communicators are already in her pack, and the language she must learn is the one that says goodbye is not the same as gone.

14. **Finale (pp. 85–105)**
The industrialists close in on the project; Nia and her friends — the ASI finally allowed to act, the spacer, the techie, and her father, who fights beside her — turn their own attack into the proof of the work's worth. The relay survives and goes public. Her father stays with his work, but this time he says goodbye properly, and Nia understands he did not leave because of her.

15. **Final Image (p. 110)**
Back beneath the walking houses, Nia gives the whale-children a jumbled, halting speech that is also a speech to herself: "Sometimes people just have to go, but that doesn't mean they're gone. Soon she'll be able to reach you." The matriarch's communicator lights up — Bali, from another world — and Nia swims with the pod without mistaking distance for loss.

**Structural Assessment:** The doubled abandonment — father and matriarch both choosing to go — gives the Midpoint and All Is Lost real emotional torque, and the communicator turns the theme into the plot's physical mechanism. The most vulnerable beat is Bad Guys Close In: the industrialists are a named placeholder with no face, no specific prize, and no reason to want the relay destroyed, so their arrival reads as plot pressure unless their goal is made concrete.
