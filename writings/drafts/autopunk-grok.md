**Beat Sheet — NIA (Feature Film, 110 pages)**

**1. Opening Image (p. 1)**
Nia, 15, underwater with her adopted orca family in the Pacific Northwest of 2109. She signs concepts through a translation interface she has refined since she was eight; a calf answers. She is fully at home in two worlds and has never questioned that both will stay.

**2. Theme Stated (p. 5)**
At the walking biobotic house her mother designed young, a neighboring group prepares to leave. “Sometimes people, and whales, just have to go,” her mother says. Nia rejects it: “Not without saying goodbye.” The line she must re-hear and re-mean is planted.

**3. Set-Up (pp. 1–10)**
Learning and work are one: invisible screens in every surface, kids contributing as they grow. Nia’s nomadic community is warm and slightly insular. We meet her enhanced techie friend and the resident embodied ASI — superintelligent comic relief who withholds answers until ignorance would kill. The pod is family; mother is love and gravity; father is absence with no farewell.

**4. Catalyst (p. 12)**
Pod members approach Nia, distressed: matriarch Bali has been missing for days — no long-range echolocation. Forced tracking chips on orcas were outlawed after sentience recognition in 2098. Nia’s scanners find nothing.

**5. Debate (pp. 12–25)**
Friend pulls satellite footage: a tanker scooping Bali from the pod. Nia demands action. Mother: “There’s nothing we can do. We’ll be moving on soon, and so will they.” Nia gathers supplies; mother finds them and tries to confine her. Want (bring Bali home) and wound (people leave without goodbye) lock.

**6. Break into Two (p. 25)**
The whales assist the escape. At the space elevator, Nia gives the pod a communicator — “if they want it, turn it on” — promises “We’ll find her,” and leaves Earth. Mother’s ASI is already hunting them.

**7. B Story (p. 30)**
ASI tags them immediately and must join rather than drag them home. They recruit a swarmy old spacer who knows which worlds can support whales. Chosen family in motion: friend, ASI, spacer — the crew that will teach Nia what staying and leaving cost.

**8. Fun and Games (pp. 30–55)**
Wormhole hops across worlds. Space whales, waiting tanks, infrastructure adapted for cetaceans; dolphins travel, humpbacks mostly stay. Misadventures with local whales; lore about a mysterious ship and the man who flies it. Nia’s translation gift thrives under pressure — the promise of the premise in full bloom.

**9. Midpoint (p. 55)**
A lead world. They find a walking house identical to home. Empty. Inside: photos of her mother, of Bali, then one of the three of them — Nia at four, mother barely older than Nia is now, and him. Her father. The man who left without goodbye. False victory: the trail ends here. Stakes flip from rescuing a whale from a stranger to confronting the man who abandoned her.

**10. Bad Guys Close In (pp. 55–75)**
Father arrives (or the house traps them and walks inland toward a fortress while friend races to override). Resentment; Nia storms. They locate Bali under guard. Pursuers close — industrialists shadowing the trail Nia blazed. Trust fractures; access narrows. Nia hardens into extraction: take Bali and leave him.

**11. All Is Lost (p. 75)**
Confrontation at the whale. Nia has secured her prize — and father says: “She wanted to come here.” Bali contacted him. The matriarch chose this work, this still place, over the matriline’s wander — the same symmetry as father leaving because the work needed roots and mother needed motion. Nia’s adopted mother-figure abandoned the pod the way her blood father abandoned her. Nothing clean left to bring home.

**12. Dark Night of the Soul (pp. 75–85)**
Alone after the reveal, Nia re-hears her mother’s line. True hurt only comes from those we love. “Bring Bali home” was her need for a goodbye she never got — not Bali’s need. She also sees what she led to this door: industrialists who would strip the work, the whale, and the project that justified years of silence.

**13. Break into Three (p. 85)**
Nia chooses neither pure rescue nor pure rejection. She will help father and Bali protect the work and defeat the industrialists — then carry the truth home in a language she has not fully mastered. Want and need realign: family is who we keep reaching, not who we cage.

**14. Finale (pp. 85–105)**
Nia, friend, ASI (finally allowed to stop teaching and start saving), spacer, father, and Bali turn the industrialist assault. The project survives; Bali stays by choice; Nia leaves a communicator so the pod will not be cut off the way she was. Father’s silence is explained by secrecy against those same industrialists — not indifference. Nia returns to Earth.

**15. Final Image (p. 110)**
In the water, Nia faces the whale-children (and herself): “Sometimes people just have to go — but that doesn’t mean they’re gone. I know you’ll miss her, but soon she’ll be able to reach you.” Optional grace note: a granddaughter’s pregnancy signals the matriline splitting and renewing. Same ocean as the opening — the wound named, the lesson lived.

---

**Structural Assessment:** The emotional architecture is strong: mother’s theme line bookends the film, the Midpoint father reveal re-aims the second half, and All Is Lost lands on chosen abandonment rather than a capture fail — correctly serving the need. Antagonistic pressure evolves from mother’s inertia to father’s secret to industrialists as the true external threat. The single most vulnerable beat is **Fun and Games (pp. 30–55)**: multi-world hops risk episodic tourism unless each stop costs Nia something specific (a mistranslation, a burned lead, a crew fracture) and plants concrete breadcrumbs to the father’s world; without that, the B Story crew and the industrialist trail feel under-motivated when they must carry Act Three.
