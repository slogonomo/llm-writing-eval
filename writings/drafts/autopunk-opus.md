# Beat Sheet — AUTOPUNK / WHALES (Feature Film, 110 pages)

**Skill:** beat-sheet-builder v1.0 · **Model:** Opus 5 (medium effort) · **Date:** 2026-08-08
**Source brief:** John's story notes (2026-08-08; not included)

**Logline:** After her pod's matriarch is captured, a nomadic 15-year-old whale-talker defies her mother and journeys off-world in pursuit, only to discover the captor is the father who abandoned her to put down roots.

**Protagonist:** Nia, 15. **Want:** find Bali and bring her home. **Need:** learn that leaving is not the same as abandoning — that love survives distance, and that holding on can drown the thing you're holding.
**Antagonist:** Structurally three-staged — her mother's *"there's nothing we can do"* (Act One), her father as presumed captor (Act Two), the industrialists Nia has unwittingly led to his door (Act Three).

---

**1. Opening Image (p. 1)**
Nia underwater at dawn, weightless among the pod, hands shaping the gestures that pair with her throat-mic translator. Bali, the matriarch, answers with a phrase Nia has spent seven years learning to hear. On the surface, the walking houses stride slowly north without her — home is always leaving; Nia is always in the water.

**2. Theme Stated (p. 5)**
Nia's mother, packing the house for the next move, tells her not to get attached to a stretch of coast: *"Everything that matters can walk."* Nia, half-listening: "Not everything wants to." The film's argument, stated by both sides in the first five pages.

**3. Set-Up (pp. 1–10)**
The community: children learning by doing, invisible interfaces waking under their palms, a window resolving into orca footage. Nia's translator is beautiful and unfinished — it renders nouns, fumbles feeling. We meet the resident embodied ASI, who knows every answer and is forbidden to give one. We establish that Nia has no memory of a father and does not ask.

**4. Catalyst (p. 12)**
Three pod members come to Nia agitated, echolocating a hole in the world. Bali is gone — days gone, past the range of any call. The translator gives Nia the word for *missing*, and then a word it cannot render at all.

**5. Debate (pp. 12–25)**
Nia's scanners find nothing; tracking chips were outlawed with the sentience ruling of 2098. Her enhanced techie friend from outside the community surfaces satellite footage: a tanker descending, scooping Bali from open water. Nia brings it to her mother. *"There's nothing we can do. We'll be moving on soon, and so will they."* — *"Sometimes people, and whales, just have to go."* — *"Not without saying goodbye."* Her mother confines her to the house.

**6. Break into Two (p. 25)**
The pod springs her — bodies against the house's legs, a distraction her mother cannot ignore. Nia and her friend run for the elevator port. At the water's edge she presses a communicator into the pod's keeping: *"Turn it on if she wants it. Don't worry — we'll find her."* She goes up.

**7. B Story (p. 30)**
The ASI catches them in orbit, sent by her mother, and simply refuses to leave. He is the film's relationship line: the being who has every answer and withholds it because being given the answer is not the same as knowing it. He is, structurally, the argument for letting go.

**8. Fun and Games (pp. 30–55)**
A swarmy old spacer who knows which worlds can hold whales takes their money and their trajectory. Wormhole hops, waiting tanks, migration stations retrofitted for whale-friends. Dolphins everywhere, humpbacks refusing to travel. At each stop Nia interviews local whales in a half-language and assembles the same rumor: a quiet ship, a man who flies it alone, a world nobody names.

**9. Midpoint (p. 55)**
They find the world — and on it, a walking house. Nia has never seen one off Earth. Inside: her mother's face in old photographs, Bali young, and one image of three people. Her mother barely older than Nia. And him. A false victory that reframes the whole hunt: she came to recover a stolen whale and has instead found the man who left without saying goodbye.

**10. Bad Guys Close In (pp. 55–75)**
The house seals and begins walking inland toward a fortress on the ridge, the friend racing the lockout as it moves; they break free late. Her father arrives — confusion, resentment, Nia storming off before he can finish a sentence. Worse: the trail she followed was legible, and something else has been following it. Ships come down through the cloud deck.

**11. All Is Lost (p. 75)**
They reach Bali. The confrontation Nia has crossed the galaxy for — and her father says: *"She wanted to come. She wants to be part of this."* Bali confirms it in her own voice, and Nia's translator holds. The matriarch chose to leave. Nia's rescue was a kidnapping in reverse; her father is not a villain; and the whistle of industrialist engines overhead means she personally led them here.

**12. Dark Night of the Soul (pp. 75–85)**
Nia alone on a strange shore. The ASI, for once, offers something — not the answer, a question: what did she think she'd bring home? She understands the symmetry: Bali left her matriline the way her father left her, and both left toward work, not away from a person. Her mother's *"nothing we can do"* was not resignation; it was the only sentence she had left after being the one who wouldn't stay.

**13. Break into Three (p. 85)**
Nia goes back and asks her father what the work is — and, for the first time, listens all the way to the end of the answer. Then she offers the one thing no one there has: she can talk to whales. The defense of the project needs the pod's cousins in this ocean, and they will only come if someone can ask.

**14. Finale (pp. 85–105)**
Nia, her friend, the ASI, the spacer, her father and Bali — and a wild pod recruited in a language Nia is still bad at — turn the industrialists' own hardware against them. The fortress is theirs. Nia stays long enough to see the project's next stage begin, and then chooses, herself, to leave, saying goodbye out loud, twice: to her father and to Bali. Bali takes a communicator.

**15. Final Image (p. 110)**
Home water. Nia gives the whale-children a jumbled, luminous speech that is also for herself: *"Sometimes people just have to go. But that doesn't mean they're gone. I know you'll miss her — but soon she'll be able to reach you."* The communicator clicks; Bali's voice crosses light-years, rough through the translator, unmistakably her. Nia's mother, in the shallows, stays put a moment longer than the house wants her to. Nearby, Bali's granddaughter is carrying a calf.

---

**Structural Assessment:** The spine is unusually sound for a first freewrite — the Midpoint discovery (a walking house where none should be) and the All Is Lost reversal (the matriarch chose to go) are cleanly differentiated, and the theme is dramatized on both a whale axis and a human axis so the ending pays two debts at once. The most vulnerable beat is **Fun and Games (pp. 30–55)**: the notes describe it as "several worlds, misadventures, collect some lore," which is 25 pages of episodic travel with no escalating antagonist and nothing at stake but information. It needs a pursuer active from p. 30 — the industrialists tracking Nia rather than discovered at p. 65 — so each world costs her something and the Act Three revelation that she led them there is a debt the audience has been watching accrue. Secondary risk: the mother exits at p. 25 and does not return until p. 110; she needs at least one contact mid-journey or her Final Image beat lands on a character we stopped knowing.

**Beats where the premise is currently thin (per skill step 4):**
- The nature of the father's work is undefined in the notes ("gotta pick one"). Beats 13 and 14 are written to hold regardless, but the Break into Three cannot be fully specific until the work has a name and a reason it must be hidden.
- Why the father could not send word in fifteen years is flagged open in the notes; Beat 12 leans on the industrialists' pursuit as the answer, which is the strongest available option but is not yet established on the page.
- Space elevator vs. ships, and the FTL/wormhole choice, are marked undecided; Beat 6 assumes an elevator port and Beat 8 assumes wormhole transit. Both are swappable without disturbing the structure.
