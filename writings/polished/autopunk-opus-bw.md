# Beat Sheet — AUTOPUNK / WHALES (Feature Film, 110 pages)

**Skill:** beat-sheet-builder v1.0 · **Model:** Opus 5 (medium effort) · **Date:** 2026-08-08
**Source brief:** John's story notes (2026-08-08; not included)
**Revision:** better-writing pass over `autopunk-opus.md`; structure and content unchanged.

**Logline:** After her pod's matriarch is captured, a nomadic 15-year-old whale-talker defies her mother and journeys off-world in pursuit, only to discover the captor is the father who abandoned her to put down roots.

**Protagonist:** Nia, 15. **Want:** find Bali and bring her home. **Need:** learn that leaving is not the same as abandoning. Love survives distance, and holding on can drown the thing you are holding.
**Antagonist:** Staged in three. Her mother's *"there's nothing we can do"* in Act One, her father as presumed captor in Act Two, and in Act Three the industrialists Nia has unwittingly led to his door.

---

**1. Opening Image (p. 1)**
Nia underwater at dawn, weightless among the pod, hands shaping the gestures that pair with her throat-mic translator. Bali, the matriarch, answers with a phrase Nia has spent seven years learning to hear. Above, on land, the walking houses stride slowly north without her. Home is always leaving. Nia is always in the water.

**2. Theme Stated (p. 5)**
Nia's mother, packing the house for the next move, tells her not to get attached to a stretch of coast: *"Everything that matters can walk."* Nia, half-listening: "Not everything wants to." Both sides of the film's argument, stated in the first five pages.

**3. Set-Up (pp. 1–10)**
The community: children learning by doing, invisible interfaces waking under their palms, a window resolving into orca footage. Nia's translator is beautiful and unfinished. It renders nouns and fumbles feeling. We meet the resident embodied ASI, who knows every answer and is forbidden to give one. Nia has no memory of a father and does not ask.

**4. Catalyst (p. 12)**
Three pod members come to Nia agitated, echolocating a hole in the world. Bali is gone. Days gone, past the range of any call. The translator gives Nia the word for *missing*, then a word it cannot render at all.

**5. Debate (pp. 12–25)**
Nia's scanners find nothing; tracking chips were outlawed with the sentience ruling of 2098. Her enhanced techie friend, from outside the community, surfaces satellite footage: a tanker descending, scooping Bali out of open water. Nia brings it to her mother. *"There's nothing we can do. We'll be moving on soon, and so will they."* — *"Sometimes people, and whales, just have to go."* — *"Not without saying goodbye."* Her mother confines her to the house.

**6. Break into Two (p. 25)**
The pod springs her: bodies thrown against the house's legs, a distraction her mother cannot ignore. Nia and her friend run for the elevator port. At the water's edge she presses a communicator into the pod's keeping. *"Turn it on if she wants it. Don't worry — we'll find her."* Then she goes up.

**7. B Story (p. 30)**
The ASI catches them in orbit, sent by her mother, and simply refuses to leave. He carries the relationship line: the being who has every answer and withholds it, because being handed the answer is not the same as knowing it. Structurally, he is the argument for letting go.

**8. Fun and Games (pp. 30–55)**
A smarmy old spacer who knows which worlds can hold whales takes their money and their trajectory. Wormhole hops. Waiting tanks. Migration stations retrofitted for whale-friends, dolphins everywhere, humpbacks that refuse to travel. At each stop Nia interviews local whales in a half-language and assembles the same rumor: a quiet ship, a man who flies it alone, a world nobody will name.

**9. Midpoint (p. 55)**
They find the world, and standing on it, a walking house. Nia has never seen one off Earth. Inside are her mother's face in old photographs, Bali young, and one image of three people: her mother barely older than Nia is now, and him. A false victory that reframes the whole hunt. She came to recover a stolen whale. She has found the man who left without saying goodbye.

**10. Bad Guys Close In (pp. 55–75)**
The house seals and starts walking inland toward a fortress on the ridge, her friend racing the lockout while the floor moves under them. They break free late. Her father arrives: confusion, resentment, Nia storming off before he can finish a sentence. Worse, the trail she followed was legible, and something else has been reading it. Ships come down through the cloud deck.

**11. All Is Lost (p. 75)**
They reach Bali. This is the confrontation Nia crossed the galaxy for, and her father says: *"She wanted to come. She wants to be part of this."* Bali confirms it in her own voice, and Nia's translator holds. The matriarch chose to leave. So Nia's rescue was a kidnapping in reverse, her father is not a villain, and the whistle of industrialist engines overhead means she led them here herself.

**12. Dark Night of the Soul (pp. 75–85)**
Nia alone on a strange shore. The ASI finally offers her something, though not the answer: a question. What did she think she was going to bring home? She sees the symmetry. Bali left her matriline the way her father left her, and both of them left toward work rather than away from a person. Her mother's *"nothing we can do"* was never resignation. It was the only sentence left to the one who wouldn't stay.

**13. Break into Three (p. 85)**
Nia goes back and asks her father what the work is, and for the first time listens all the way to the end of an answer. Then she offers the one thing nobody there has: she can talk to whales. Defending the project needs the pod's cousins in this ocean, and they will only come if someone can ask them.

**14. Finale (pp. 85–105)**
Nia, her friend, the ASI, the spacer, her father, Bali, and a wild pod recruited in a language Nia is still bad at turn the industrialists' own hardware against them. The fortress is theirs. Nia stays long enough to see the project's next stage begin, then chooses, herself, to leave, and says goodbye out loud, twice: to her father, and to Bali. Bali takes a communicator.

**15. Final Image (p. 110)**
Home water. Nia gives the whale-children a jumbled, luminous speech that is also for herself: *"Sometimes people just have to go. But that doesn't mean they're gone. I know you'll miss her — but soon she'll be able to reach you."* The communicator clicks. Bali's voice crosses light-years, rough through the translator, unmistakably her. In the shallows, Nia's mother stays put a moment longer than the house wants her to. Nearby, Bali's granddaughter is carrying a calf.

**Structural assessment**

The spine is unusually sound for a first freewrite. The Midpoint discovery (a walking house where none should be) and the All Is Lost reversal (the matriarch chose to go) are cleanly differentiated, and the theme is dramatized on a whale axis and a human axis at once, so the ending pays two debts instead of one.

The weak beat is **Fun and Games (pp. 30–55)**. The notes describe it as "several worlds, misadventures, collect some lore," which is 25 pages of episodic travel with no escalating antagonist and nothing at stake but information. It needs a pursuer working from p. 30: the industrialists tracking Nia, not discovered at p. 65. Then each world costs her something, and the Act Three revelation that she led them there settles a debt the audience has watched accrue.

Second problem, smaller but real. The mother exits at p. 25 and does not return until p. 110. She needs at least one contact mid-journey, or her Final Image beat lands on a character we stopped knowing.

**Beats where the premise is currently thin (per skill step 4):**
- The nature of the father's work is undefined in the notes ("gotta pick one"). Beats 13 and 14 are written to hold either way, but Break into Three cannot be fully specific until the work has a name and a reason it must be hidden.
- Why the father could not send word in fifteen years is flagged open in the notes. Beat 12 leans on the industrialists' pursuit as the answer, which is the strongest option available and is not yet established on the page.
- Space elevator versus ships, and the FTL/wormhole choice, are marked undecided. Beat 6 assumes an elevator port and Beat 8 assumes wormhole transit. Both are swappable without disturbing the structure.
