**Beat Sheet — WHALES (Feature Film, 110 pages)**

**1. Opening Image (p. 1)**
In 2109, fifteen-year-old Nia swims coastal Pacific waters beside her adopted orca pod, fine-tuning a prototype translation collar. On the beach, her mother’s biobotic walking house steps along the tide line on eight mechanical legs. Nia trades clear, two-way speech with Bali, the pod’s elder matriarch.

**2. Theme Stated (p. 5)**
While repairing the house's ambient display shell, Nia’s mother sees Nia tracking the pod’s migration vectors on her wrist monitor. Her mother offers a hard truth: "Sometimes people, and whales, just have to go. You can't force what you love to stay frozen in place." Nia rejects it outright. Family stays.

**3. Set-Up (pp. 1–10)**
Earth in the wake of the Great Awakening: education, labor, and ecological maintenance blend into daily routines. Nia has spent seven years refining human-orca translation, driven by the memory of a father who disappeared without a word when she was eight. Her mother lives as a detached wanderer, keeping the walking house moving down the coast, but Nia grounds her life in the daily rhythm of the pod.

**4. Catalyst (p. 12)**
Distressed orcas crowd Nia, reporting that matriarch Bali went silent on long-range echolocation. Nia and her tech-enhanced friend hack into orbital surveillance feeds, uncovering classified video of a low-orbit transport vessel scooping Bali from the ocean and burning into upper atmosphere.

**5. Debate (pp. 12–25)**
Nia begs her mother to trace the vessel, but her mother tells her the pod will adapt and orders her to let it go. Nia and her friend pack travel gear in secret. When her mother catches them loading supplies, she locks down the walking house, confining Nia inside to stop a reckless launch into deep space.

**6. Break into Two (p. 25)**
The pod's younger orcas slam against the house's submerged coastal sensors, tricking the security system into triggering a flood override that unlatches the doors. Nia drops a long-range sub-space communicator into the water, vows to return with Bali, and slips aboard a cargo climber on the nearest space elevator.

**7. B Story (p. 30)**
Nia’s mother dispatches the family's education ASI, a witty, rule-bound droid that answers questions with counter-questions. Catching the teens at the orbital hub, the droid calculates that forcing them home will backfire. Instead, it hires an old spacer familiar with off-world cetacean routes to escort them, leaving Nia to make tactical decisions herself.

**8. Fun and Games (pp. 30–55)**
The crew hops jump gates across ocean moons and orbital hatcheries, translating dialects for local dolphin colonies to follow the freighter's trajectory. Nia adapts her collar to work across exotic aquatic biomes, while an industrialist syndicate quietly monitors her signal log across the sector.

**9. Midpoint (p. 55)**
False victory. Nia tracks the freighter to a remote sanctuary moon and finds a walking house built to her mother’s precise design specifications. Breaking into the bay to release Bali, Nia discovers holo-logs showing her mother, her missing father, and Bali working together years ago. The "captor" is her father, and Bali transferred off Earth willingly to help him.

**10. Bad Guys Close In (pp. 55–75)**
Her father explains his departure: his research into multi-species neural nets required permanent facilities, a life her nomadic mother refused to share. Before Nia can process the revelation, the industrialist syndicate, having followed her telemetry, lands armed extraction teams to claim the neural technology and harvest Bali for commercial bio-processing.

**11. All Is Lost (p. 75)**
Mercenaries blow the compound doors, steal the research core, and winch Bali into an armored haul tank. Nia’s father takes a blast protecting the console, her friend's gear short-circuits, and Nia sees that her rush to rescue a whale who left on purpose brought an armed fleet down on everyone she loves.

**12. Dark Night of the Soul (pp. 75–85)**
In the wreckage of the laboratory, Nia bandages her father alongside the droid. She sees what her mother meant on the shoreline: holding onto someone does not mean chaining them in place. Forgiving her father for leaving, she stops trying to control where her family stays.

**13. Break into Three (p. 85)**
Nia remembers the sub-space beacon she gave her pod on Earth. Routing her translation collar through the sanctuary's planetary solar array, she boosts the signal across light-years. The ASI unlocks its combat subroutines to help, and Nia coordinates her team and a local dolphin pod for an ambush at the orbital jump gate.

**14. Finale (pp. 85–105)**
Nia leads a two-pronged strike against the flagship. She broadcasts tactical commands to local space-cetaceans to disable the freighter’s gravity drives while her friend overrides the lock systems. Nia boards the deck, confronts the corporate commander, and blows the release latches on Bali's haul tank. Working alongside her father and the cetacean pods, she disables the mercenary fleet and secures the moon.

**15. Final Image (p. 110)**
Back home, Nia swims in Pacific waters while her mother's walking house rests on the sand. Nia powers up an interstellar relay, opening a continuous audio channel between the Earth pod and Bali across space. Looking out at the ocean beside her mother, Nia says to the young orcas: "Sometimes people just have to go... but that doesn't mean they're gone."

---

**Structural Assessment:** The 15-beat structure turns a personal rescue mission into a clean coming-of-age arc, anchored by strong shifts at the Midpoint and All Is Lost beats. The main vulnerability is Break into Two (p. 25): having the young orcas trigger the house's flood override risks feeling like a plot contrivance unless Nia's translation collar and a deliberate signal sequence are established early as an intentional trick.
