# Beat Sheet — Whales (working title)

**Skill:** beat-sheet-builder · **Model:** Kimi K3 (moonshotai/kimi-k3) via Nousportal · **Date:** 2026-08-08
**Format:** Feature film (~110 pages)
**Logline:** After her pod's matriarch is captured, a nomadic 15-year-old whale-talker defies her mother and journeys off-world in pursuit, only to discover the captor is the father who abandoned her to put down roots.
**Protagonist:** Nia, 15. Want: find Bali, the kidnapped orca matriarch, and bring her home. Need: learn that family, born and adopted, survives departure. Sometimes people just have to go; that doesn't mean they're gone.
**Antagonistic force:** Her father (the "captor"), revealed to be sheltering Bali's chosen work; then the industrialists Nia unknowingly leads to his door. At home: her mother's "there's nothing we can do."

---

**1. Opening Image (p. 1)**
Nia, 15, free-dives off the Pacific Northwest coast in 2109, fully at ease inside an orca pod's hunting formation. Through a wrist-mounted translation rig she trades clipped concepts with a young male; she has been improving this system since she was 8. She surfaces laughing, at home in the water in a way she never is on land.

**2. Theme Stated (p. 5)**
Inside the walking biobotic house, Nia's mother watches the pod through a window-turned-display and says, almost to herself: "Sometimes people, and whales, just have to go." Nia barely registers it. It will become the sentence she fights for two acts and finally understands.

**3. Set-Up (pp. 1–10)**
Nia's world: a nomadic community in walking houses her mother designed, children learning through invisible screens woven into every surface, the pod as extended family. We see her mother's quiet authority, Nia's devotion to Bali the matriarch, and the one subject nobody raises: the father who left without goodbye. Nia is defined by belonging; the wound underneath is abandonment.

**4. Catalyst (p. 12)**
Pod members approach Nia in visible distress: Bali is missing. Even long-range echolocation finds nothing. Days of silence from the pod's center. Nia's own scanners confirm it. The matriarch is gone.

**5. Debate (pp. 12–25)**
Nia enlists an enhanced-techie friend from outside the community. Tracking chips were outlawed when orca sentience was recognized, so they pull satellite footage instead: a tanker descending over the ocean, scooping Bali up and away. Nia brings it to her mother and hits the wall: "There's nothing we can do. We'll be moving on soon, and so will they." Nia refuses to accept it. Her mother catches her packing supplies and confines her to the house.

**6. Break into Two (p. 25)**
The pod helps Nia escape, a coordinated distraction at the waterline while she and her friend slip away. At the shipping port she pauses, gives the pod a communicator ("turn it on if you want it"), and promises: "Don't worry, we'll find her." She boards an off-world transit, leaving the only world she's known.

**7. B Story (p. 30)**
Her mother sends the community's resident embodied ASI to retrieve the kids: a superintelligent, wryly comic guide constitutionally barred from handing them answers (until ignorance is about to kill them). He fails to bring them home and tags along instead. He becomes the story's second spine: the mentor who teaches Nia that understanding must be earned, not received.

**8. Fun and Games (pp. 30–55)**
Nia, her friend, and the ASI hop worlds through wormhole transit with a grizzled old spacer who knows which worlds can support whales. Space-going dolphins can't get enough of the lanes; humpbacks refuse them. Each stop yields lore about a mysterious ship and the man who flies it, and quietly sharpens Nia's translation craft as she questions whales whose minds work nothing like her pod's. Intercut: a corporate vessel logging her route.

**9. Midpoint (p. 55)**
The trail lands on an unnamed world, and Nia sees a walking house. Her mother's design, where it cannot possibly be. Inside: old photos of her mother, of Bali, and then one of three people: her mother, barely older than Nia is now, a baby Nia, and him. Her father. He arrives. The captor she's crossed a solar system to fight is the man who left. She storms out, a false peak that collapses into fury.

**10. Bad Guys Close In (pp. 55–75)**
Pursuers close on the valley: the industrialists who have been following her signal since her first off-world query. Her father reveals what "this" is. Bali contacted him; the matriarch chose to come, for work that matters to both species, work that requires him to stay still, the one thing Nia's mother could never do. Nia rejects it. Every step of her pursuit has been a beacon leading the worst people in the system to his door.

**11. All Is Lost (p. 75)**
The industrialists breach the sanctuary's perimeter: tanks, grapples, the same technology that took Bali, now aimed at the entire project. Nia stands in the middle of it, translator in hand, and finally hears what Bali has been saying all along: *I chose this.* The matriarch abandoned her pod exactly as her father abandoned her. The symmetry is unbearable. Her quest's premise, rescue the victim, is dead.

**12. Dark Night of the Soul (pp. 75–85)**
Alone at the water's edge of the holding bay, Nia faces the return trip she now dreads: going home to tell her pod, in a language she still hasn't mastered, that their mother left them on purpose. She replays her mother's sentence, "sometimes people just have to go," and for the first time hears it not as surrender but as love that doesn't chain. She thinks of the communicator she gave the pod: *if they want it.* Choice, not capture. She knows what she has to do.

**13. Break into Three (p. 85)**
Nia goes back in, not to take Bali, but to defend her. She brings the one asset the industrialists can't match: two species talking fluently, in real time. She proposes the fight on the whales' terms.

**14. Finale (pp. 85–105)**
Nia, her friend, the ASI, the spacer, her father, and Bali's coordinated pod-allies turn the industrialists' own capture infrastructure against them. The sanctuary's tanks and tidegates become a trap sprung in a language the attackers can't hear. The work is secured; the project launches its next stage with Bali as willing ambassador. Nia says a real goodbye this time, to her father, who finally gets to say it back, and to Bali, who accepts a second communicator.

**15. Final Image (p. 110)**
Back home, Nia floats in the water with the pod's whale-children and delivers a touching, jumbled speech that is also to herself: "Sometimes people just have to go, but that doesn't mean they're gone. You'll miss her, but soon she'll be able to reach you." Behind her, a young female lingers at the surface, newly pregnant, the matriline continuing. Nia dives. Same water, same girl, but now she swims beside her family, not inside the illusion that love means staying.

---

**Structural Assessment:** The architecture is strong. The Midpoint reveal (captor = father) and the All Is Lost reveal (Bali chose to go) are cleanly differentiated escalations, and the theme lands at all three required beats. The most vulnerable beat is Break into Three: Nia's pivot from rescuer to defender happens in a single decision, and the story hasn't yet earned why the industrialists can be beaten at all. The Finale's "fight on the whales' terms" needs a planted capability or cost earlier in Fun and Games, or the third act risks solving itself by sentiment rather than strategy. (John's bracketed open questions, the nature of the father's work, why he couldn't be in contact, must also be answered before this beat can carry its weight.)
